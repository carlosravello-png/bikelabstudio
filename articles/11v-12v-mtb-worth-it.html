11 speeds vs 12 speeds in MTB: real analysis of watts, friction and 5-year cost

February 2026 — Friction losses and 5-year cost analysis. No hype. No Instagram opinions. No shop lies.

1. Watts: 11v vs 12v difference

Smallest Cog: 10T vs 11T

10T (12v) loses 1.1–1.3 W more than 11T (11v) at 200–300 W power output. Lab-measured maximum: 1.5 W. Minimum: 0.5 W.

Large Cogs ≥18T (Climbs)

At cogs ≥18T — where you spend 70–80% of your time on real climbs — the difference is 0 W. Both systems are identical.

Total Impact

Approximately 0.5% of the power you produce. Example: a 3-hour ride at 200 W average means 0.5–1 W less lost with 12v. In practice, you do not notice it: not on long climbs, not on technical trail.

2. Friction and range: the 12v +10% almost nobody uses

12v delivers 510% range vs 463.6% for 11v. That 10% extra is concentrated in large cogs (high speed, downhill pedalling).

In typical Peruvian MTB terrain — technical climbs, mixed trails, rocky ground — you barely use that zone. Real benefit ≈ 0%.

3. 5-year cost: 11v vs 12v maintenance

Assuming 3,000 km/year and normal maintenance:

Factor 11 speeds 12 speeds
Range 463.6% 510%
5-year total cost $533 $778
Real energy difference Reference ~0.5% (smallest cog)
Maintenance increase — +46%

Initial investment: 11v $145 | 12v $190 → +$45

Chains (7.5 vs 10): 11v $225 | 12v $350 → +$125

Cassettes (2.5 units): 11v $163 | 12v $238 → +$75

Total 11v: $533 | Total 12v: $778

Difference: +$245 (+46%)

The real difference is approximately 1 watt — but only when you use the smallest cog. If you use that cog 5% of the time, on a 3-hour ride you are "saving" that watt for only 9 minutes. Over the entire session, the actual average improvement is approximately 0.06 W.

That is less than normal power-meter variance; less than 1 psi off on a tyre; less than a small pacing error on a climb. You are paying $245 extra over 5 years for an improvement that, in practice, is imperceptible. To extend your drivetrain life, see chain and 0.5% limit.

4. 12v Upgrade: Engineering or Commercial Trick?

Upgrading to 12 speeds has clear technical logic in specific scenarios; but outside those it often becomes marginal expense disguised as an essential improvement.

Professional Scenario

In high-level competition (World Cup XC/Marathon), where every watt and second count, the 12v range allows maintaining optimal cadence in aggressive transitions. There, the additional 46% maintenance cost is operational expense assumed by teams or elite athletes.

The Reality of the Amateur Ecosystem in Peru

In regional or provincial races, and in many local workshops, the ecosystem works like this: the race organizer often runs a bike shop or spare-parts business. That is not bad in itself — without them there would be no events or logistics — but it creates a natural conflict of interest: the same person who designs the route and awards prizes is the one suggesting 12v upgrades as "necessary" to "perform better".

What Repeats in the Circuit

  • A complete new or second-hand system (cassette, chain, derailleur) is offered with the promise of "high performance" and "more pro", without mentioning that the real cost is +46% in maintenance over 5 years for only 0.5% measurable energy gain.
  • It is omitted that installing it on an old hub often requires de-lacing and re-lacing the wheel — weakening the structural tension of the rim — or buying a new hub, with additional expense.
  • Real compatibility is ignored (critical micron tolerances that 12v does not forgive), and the "pro" badge is sold with no demonstrable gain in watts or on the trail.

A well-optimized 11v system covers 95% of requirements on Peruvian trails. That is rarely mentioned when the priority is commercial.

It is not that 12v is bad; it is that the "essential upgrade" is sold more for business than for engineering. The rider ends up paying for an extra margin that does not translate into real performance.

5. Analysis Limitations

This analysis is theoretical: clean chain, perfect lubrication, zero dust, constant load. In real conditions — fine desert dust, Andean mud, variable altitude, drivetrain maintenance — total losses increase significantly and the difference between 11v and 12v almost disappears.

6. Conclusion: Is 12v Worth It?

It depends on your use, budget, and tolerance for marginal expense. The technical difference exists, but it is small and costly. It is not essential for most riders.
